Converting images to PDF is useful for sharing photo collections, submitting documents, or creating a portfolio. The challenge is doing it without degrading image quality. When images are carelessly converted, the result can look blurry, pixelated, or have visible compression artifacts.

The key to preserving quality is understanding how images are embedded in a PDF. When you add an image to a PDF, it can be stored as-is or recompressed. Storing the original image data without recompression preserves full quality but results in a larger file. Recompressing with JPEG reduces size but introduces artifacts.

For maximum quality, use a tool that embeds images at their original resolution. The images-to-PDF converter on Go LANA takes your uploaded images and places each one on its own page at full resolution. This means the PDF will look exactly like the original images.

When preparing images for conversion, consider the page size. Standard PDF pages are letter size (8.5 x 11 inches) or A4. If your images have different aspect ratios, they may have white margins or be scaled to fit. For edge-to-edge images, crop or resize them to match the target page ratio before converting.

Image format matters too. PNG images are lossless and will preserve every pixel. JPEG images are already compressed, so embedding them in a PDF without further compression keeps the original quality. WebP images are converted to a compatible format during the process.

If file size is a concern but you still want good quality, aim for 150-200 DPI for documents that will be printed and 72-96 DPI for screen-only viewing. Most monitors display at 96 DPI, so anything higher is wasted for digital viewing but important for print.

To convert multiple images into a single PDF, arrange them in the desired order before starting. Most converters let you drag and reorder pages. Once converted, you can use a PDF merger to combine the result with other PDF pages if needed.

Always preview the final PDF at 100% zoom to check image quality. If any page looks soft, go back and use a higher resolution version of that image. The goal is a PDF that looks identical to viewing the original images directly.
